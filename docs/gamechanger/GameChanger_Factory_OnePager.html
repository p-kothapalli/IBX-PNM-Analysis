The GameChanger Factory

The whole multi-agentic SDLC, on one page
GameChanger 3.0 IBC Delivery

The problem. You hand an AI a story and say “build this.” What comes back reads well, compiles, and references a field called PRM_PractitionerStatus__c that does not exist in your org. Nobody notices until deploy day — and here is the worst part: nothing failed. There was no step that went wrong, because there were never any steps. One big answer: trust all of it, or throw all of it away.

The factory analogy — this is the system don’t ask one clever person for a car — build a factory
1
The assembly line has ten stations.

Station 1 reads the customer’s order. Station 2 writes the plan. Station 3 draws the blueprint. Station 4 writes the test checklist… Station 10 packs the manual in the glovebox. Each station does one job and writes exactly one note. If the car is wrong, you can walk the line and point at the station that did it.

2
Next to eight stations stands an inspector with a clipboard.

The inspector builds nothing. It scores the note, and if the score is too low it says “do it again” and hands it back with the reasons circled in red. That’s a critic — verdicts are ACCEPT, ACCEPT_WITH_CAVEATS, REFINE_AGAIN, ESCALATE_TO_HUMAN.

3
Thirteen more people work in the building but are not on the line.

One walks to the car park with a tape measure and writes down what the real cars look like. Three read the order and argue about whether it makes sense before a bolt is turned. Five gather the paperwork, check it’s complete, score it, decide who signs, and staple it into a folder for the board. Three work after the car rolls off. One is a specialist for a different vehicle entirely, who stays home unless you call him.

4
One rule outranks all others: if the blueprint and the real car disagree, the real car wins.

Always. A two-week-old planning document does not get to overrule what the org actually contains today.

5
And the twist: the stations don’t run themselves.

The factory prints a job card for each station and hangs it up. Then you walk in — in Cursor — and the AI in your editor works the job cards with live access to the real org, so it can check its own claims. Then the factory collects the finished notes and validates them.

Critic tier on the corner badge — F full · S standard · L lightweight contract check. Stations 04–07 declare 03-tdd as their rework target: a weak test checklist doesn’t fail once, it comes back at you four stations later.

How a run actually executes three phases, with a human in the middle
Phase 1
Context assembly
A CLI command

The factory prints the job cards and hangs them up. Writes one .task.json per stage per story, plus the org snapshot — then stops.

Phase 2
Generation
You, in Cursor — no command

You walk in and work each station with the real car in front of you. The agent runs every pendingVerifications query before writing a field-level claim.

Phase 3
Extraction
A CLI command

The factory collects the notes, rejects any that are blank or still a placeholder, and closes the job out into an extraction packet.

Why it works this way: the writing agent needs live tools — it must run SOQL and read the repo while it writes. A detached API call could not verify its own claims, which would defeat the whole design. There is no API-key fallback.

The house rule

When two sources disagree, precedence decides — not recency, not which one the model found convincing.

0authoritative — live org metadata & SOQL · wins everything
1scoping — contract packs · bounds scope only
2guidance — standards, reference docs
3persona — role profiles, tone
4background — narrative colour · never decisive

Per claim, the same idea: KP bundle → live SOQL → Jira/Confluence → [Inferred: UNVERIFIED]. There is no fifth option — every claim traces to something outside the model, or is explicitly admitted as unverified.

The two questions this design answers.  “How do I know it didn’t invent that field?” — every stage climbs the verification ladder, and an unproven claim must be labelled [Inferred: UNVERIFIED] with an open question attached.  “What happens when a stage gets it wrong?” — a critic scores it and returns REFINE_AGAIN, and the stage re-runs with the feedback attached.
